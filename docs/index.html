1

A single agentic rollout

A tool-using agent is given a task.

At each step, the agent takes an action: a tool call or a message to the user.

The environment responds with an observation: a reply from the user or the output of a tool.

One rollout produces a long sequence of actions and observations…

…until the agent decides it has finished the task.

A grader then checks the final state to determine whether the agent succeeded. Here, it failed.

Could we recover the outcome without the grader?

Can the outcome be read from the actions and observations alone?

2

Where did it go wrong?

Somewhere in its rollout, the agent took an action or received an observation that led to its failure.

We can rewind to an earlier step and resample from there to see whether the outcome changes.

If completions resampled from the same prefix both succeed and fail, the agent is still stochastic at that point: its fate isn’t sealed.

Resampling from enough points would map out every path the agent could take on this task. But the number of rollouts this requires is far too large in practice.

What we can afford is a few dozen independent rollouts of each task from the same agent, which is exactly what GRPO samples during RL post-training.

Many steps are nearly identical across rollouts: the same action, or the same question phrased differently.

By clustering similar steps across rollouts into shared states, every rollout becomes a path through a map of the agent’s behavior on the task.

Each rollout’s success or failure marks the states and connections it passes through, showing which parts of the map lead to success and which to failure.

We hypothesize that treating an agent’s rollouts on a task as a map of its behavior reveals which actions and observations predict how a rollout will end.

3

The finding.

0

AUROC, best per-task classifier, held-out rollouts

Fit a nearest-centroid or an RBF classifier on one task's rollouts, or a fastText model on its state sequences, and it predicts the outcomes of rollouts it has not seen. Across three benchmarks and three policies.

Successes and failures separate along the path.

Every task, though, needs its own classifier. ↓

4

The obstacle.

0

labeled rollouts per task, and none of it carries over

A per-task classifier is capped by the labeled rollouts one task provides, and it knows nothing about the next task. Shrink the labels and it falls apart; change the task and it starts from zero.

Per-task fitting transfers nothing.

So learn the representation instead. ↓

5

The idea.

0

dimensions per rollout, or any prefix of it

Precog-RM embeds each action and observation, projects them, and reads the whole sequence with a small transformer; mean pooling gives one point. It is meta-learned across many tasks and policies so that rollouts heading the same way land near each other.

On a new task, one batch of labeled rollouts becomes two prototypes, the mean of its passes and the mean of its failures. A rollout is scored by distance to them. Nothing is fitted at inference.

A rollout becomes a point; a batch becomes two prototypes.

How few rollouts? How far does it carry? ↓

6

The proof.

0

labeled rollouts per class match the per-task classifiers at eight

One encoder separates rollouts of policies it never saw; across benchmarks the transfer is partial and recovers once the benchmark joins the pool.

Trained on prefixes too, it scores a rollout while it runs. Gate on confidence and the prediction becomes a decision.

Two per class. Unseen policies. Mid-rollout.

What is it good for? ↓

7

The payoff.

0

fewer training FLOPs, pass@1 within one std of baseline

Inside GRPO, rollouts that cross the confidence threshold are terminated and handed the predicted outcome as their reward. The optimizer is untouched.

Stop the rollouts that are already decided.

Back to the thesis. ↓

8

A representation that keeps the outcome, and carries it.

Same-task outcomes are separable in embedded action–observation sequences; Precog-RM carries that signal to unseen tasks and policies from a single batch; it predicts mid-rollout and abstains when the signal is absent; and as a GRPO controller it cuts training FLOPs by up to 47.3% at near-baseline pass@1.

Now run it again on a task you choose.
